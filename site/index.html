Method

The network. Every road, path and trail from OpenStreetMap, split at intersections and checked for who may use it in which direction: one-way streets, bike lanes, footpaths, roads closed to cars.

The ingredients. Each stretch of road is measured against:

  • Water: distance to the sea, lakes and rivers.
  • Parks & forest and farmland within about 60–100 m.
  • Street trees from the city tree inventories.
  • Sights: viewpoints, heritage places, lighthouses, landmarks with a Wikipedia article.
  • Stops: ice cream, cafés, bakeries, farm stands, picnic sites, beaches, washrooms.
  • The road itself: posted speed and lanes (slower and two lanes is best), traffic signals per kilometre, gentle curves.
  • Bike lanes and paths, or quiet streets for walking.
  • Industrial land counts against.

Tiers. A road's score is the weighted mix, and tiers are relative to the region: S is the top 5% of road length, A the next 10%, then B 15%, C 20% and D the rest.

Routing. The planner finds the quickest path where every second on a low-rated road costs extra; the Direct ↔ Scenic slider sets how much. Loops pick two high-rated stretches at the right distance and route through them.

Honest limitations

  • There is no ground truth for "a nice drive", so the scores are a transparent recipe, not a trained model. The weights are yours to change.
  • OpenStreetMap is only as complete as local mappers have made it. Missing speed limits fall back to the BC default of 50 km/h, and lane counts are often guessed from the road type.
  • Traffic isn't included: a lovely road at 3 pm on a sunny Sunday may be a queue.
  • Street trees come from city inventories, which cover Victoria, Esquimalt and Vancouver only, so tree-lined roads elsewhere are under-rated.
  • Opening hours aren't checked: phone ahead before driving out for ice cream.
